Lesson 1.5a: Gas Pressure and the Gas Laws (氣壓和氣體定律)

Why does a sealed bottle of water brought down from a plane come back squashed? Why must tyre pressure be checked when the tyres are cold? Three simple laws describe how a gas behaves.

Idea 1

Gases exert pressure

Pressure ⭐Pressure = normal force ÷ area: P = F/A
Unit: pascal (Pa) = N m⁻² (1 kPa = 1000 Pa)

Gas molecules keep hitting the walls of their container; each collision pushes on the wall. The force per unit area is the gas pressure. It acts equally in all directions and is measured with a Bourdon gauge (or a pressure sensor).

The air around us exerts atmospheric pressure: about 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa at sea level (1 atm) — about 10 N on every cm²!

🔬 Evidence for air pressure
  • Collapsing can: boil a little water in a metal can, seal it and cool it. The steam condenses, the pressure inside drops, and the atmosphere crushes the can.
  • Drinking through a straw: you lower the pressure inside the straw; atmospheric pressure pushes the drink up.
  • A rubber suction hook sticks to a smooth wall.
✏️ ExampleA 10 N force pushes a light piston (area 4.0 cm²) into a cylinder of air. Atmospheric pressure = 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa. The pressure inside balances the outside:
Pin = Patm + F/A = 1.0 × 10⁵ + 10 ÷ (4.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 1.25 × 10⁵ Pa
Common mistake ⚠️Forgetting to convert cm² to m² (1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m²) and cm³ to m³ (1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³).
Idea 2

Boyle’s law: pressure and volume

🔬 ExperimentConnect a syringe of air (30 cm³) to a Bourdon gauge with short rubber tubing. Push the piston in slowly, 5 cm³ at a time, recording V and P. Plot P against V and P against 1/V.
Precautions: short tubing (air in the tubing is not measured); sealed connections (fixed mass of gas); push slowly and do not hold the syringe in your hand (keep the temperature constant).
Boyle’s law ⭐For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, the pressure is inversely proportional to the volume:
P ∝ 1/V, i.e. PV = constant ⇒ P₁V₁ = P₂V₂
✏️ ExampleA sealed bottle holds air at 76 kPa in an aircraft cabin. On landing the pressure is 100 kPa (same temperature). Vafter/Vbefore = 76/100 = 0.76 — the bottle is squashed.

Idea 3

Pressure and temperature; absolute zero

🔬 ExperimentA flask of dry air, connected by short tubing to a Bourdon gauge, is completely immersed in a water bath. Heat the water gently and stir; record P at different temperatures θ. (Fixed mass, fixed volume.)

The graph of P against θ (in °C) is a straight line that does not pass through the origin. Extrapolated backwards it meets P = 0 at about −273 °C, whatever gas or amount is used.

Absolute zero and the kelvin ⭐−273 °C is absolute zero, the lowest possible temperature (the molecules would have the least possible energy, so a gas would exert no pressure). The Kelvin scale starts there:
T/K = θ/°C + 273  (a change of 1 K = a change of 1 °C)
Pressure law ⭐For a fixed mass at constant volume: P ∝ T (T in kelvin) ⇒ P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂
Common mistakes ⚠️
  • Using °C: heating a gas from 20 °C to 40 °C does not double its pressure (293 K → 313 K: only about 7% more).
  • Converting wrongly: 27 °C = 300 K, not 246 K; 0 K = −273 °C.
Idea 4

Volume and temperature (Charles’ law)

🔬 ExperimentA sealed syringe (lubricated piston, dry air) is immersed in a water bath; the piston moves freely so the pressure stays at atmospheric pressure. Heat and stir the water; record V at different θ.

V against θ is a straight line which, extrapolated, meets V = 0 at −273 °C too — another way to find absolute zero.

Charles’ law ⭐For a fixed mass at constant pressure: V ∝ T ⇒ V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂
✏️ ExampleA closed soft plastic bottle contains 750 cm³ of air at 25 °C. It is put in a fridge at 4 °C (pressure stays atmospheric).
V₂ = V₁ × T₂/T₁ = 750 × (4 + 273)/(25 + 273) = 750 × 277/298 = 697 cm³ — the bottle shrinks a little.

Idea 5

Combining the laws: PV/T = constant and PV = nRT

Combining P ∝ 1/V (constant T), P ∝ T (constant V) and V ∝ T (constant P) for a fixed mass of gas:

⭐PV/T = constant ⇒ P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂

The constant is proportional to the amount of gas, measured in moles. 1 mole contains NA = 6.02 × 10²³ particles: n = N/NA. The mass of n moles is nM, where M is the molar mass (kg mol⁻¹).

General gas law ⭐PV = nRT, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹
P in Pa, V in m³, T in K, n in mol
Checklist ⚠️
  • Temperature in kelvin — always add 273.
  • Volume in m³ (1 L = 10⁻³ m³, 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³); pressure in Pa.
  • Use P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ only for a fixed mass. If gas leaks or is pumped in, use PV = nRT to find n before and after.
  • Pressure from a Bourdon gauge or a reading “above atmospheric” must be converted to the actual pressure.
Worked examples

Gas law problems

✏️ Example 1: helium balloonAt sea level: 100 kPa, 25 °C. At 2000 m: 60 kPa, 10 °C. Percentage change in volume?
V₂/V₁ = (P₁/P₂)(T₂/T₁) = (100/60)(283/298) = 1.583 ⇒ volume increases by 58.3%
✏️ Example 2: gas leakingAn oxygen cylinder (0.050 m³) at 27 °C: the pressure falls from 5.0 × 10⁵ Pa to 3.0 × 10⁵ Pa. M(O₂) = 0.032 kg mol⁻¹. Mass of oxygen used?
Δn = ΔP V/(RT) = (2.0 × 10⁵)(0.050) ÷ (8.31 × 300) = 4.01 mol
mass = 4.01 × 0.032 = 0.128 kg
✏️ Example 3: molecules in a roomHow many molecules are in 1.0 m³ of air at 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa and 0 °C?
n = PV/(RT) = (1.01 × 10⁵)(1.0) ÷ (8.31 × 273) = 44.5 mol ⇒ N = nNA = 44.5 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.68 × 10²⁵
Units check: Pa × m³ = N m⁻² × m³ = N m = J, and J ÷ (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ × K) = mol ✓
