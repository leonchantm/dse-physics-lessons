Lesson 1.5b: Kinetic Theory of Gases (氣體分子運動論)

Lesson 1.5a told us how gases behave (the gas laws). Kinetic theory explains why, using billions of tiny molecules flying about and bouncing off the walls.

Idea 1

Gas molecules are in random motion

🔬 Smoke cellFill a small glass cell with smoke, light it from the side and look through a microscope (or a video camera). The bright smoke particles jiggle about randomly. They are hit unevenly from all sides by invisible, fast-moving air molecules moving in random directions.

Gas molecules move in all directions with a range of speeds, colliding with each other and with the walls. At room temperature air molecules move at about 500 m s⁻¹ on average!

🔬 Mechanical modelA tube contains small beads with a light piston on top; a vibrator at the bottom makes the beads move randomly.
ModelGas
beadsmolecules
height of pistonvolume
weight on pistonpressure
speed of vibrator (beads)temperature
Faster vibrator → piston pushed higher (gas expands when heated). More weight → piston lower (higher pressure, smaller volume).
Idea 2

Gas pressure comes from molecular bombardment

Pressure ⭐When a molecule hits a wall and rebounds, its momentum changes, so the wall exerts a force on it — and by Newton’s third law it exerts a force on the wall. Huge numbers of collisions every second give a steady average force; the force per unit area is the gas pressure.
Pressure is larger when the molecules hit the walls more frequently and/or more vigorously (faster).
Change (fixed mass of gas)Kinetic-theory explanation ⭐
compressed at constant T → P increases (Boyle)same average speed, but molecules are more crowded, so they hit the walls more frequently.
heated at constant V → P increases (pressure law)average K.E. and speed increase, so molecules hit the walls more frequently and more vigorously.
heated at constant P → V increases (expansion)faster molecules hit the walls more frequently and vigorously and push the walls (piston) outwards until the collision rate falls enough for the pressure to match the outside again.
Common mistakes ⚠️
  • “When a gas is compressed at constant temperature the molecules move faster.” ✗ Same temperature → same average speed; they only hit more often.
  • “The molecules expand when the gas is heated.” ✗ The molecules don’t get bigger; the spaces between them increase.

Idea 3

Assumptions of the kinetic model of an ideal gas

Assumptions ⭐
  1. A gas consists of a very large number of molecules in random motion.
  2. The volume of the molecules is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
  3. There are no intermolecular forces except during collisions.
  4. Collisions between molecules and with the walls are perfectly elastic (no loss of K.E.).
  5. The duration of a collision is negligible compared with the time between collisions.
  6. The effect of gravity on the molecules is negligible.
Not an assumption ⚠️The model does not assume that all molecules have the same speed — they have a wide range of speeds (see the speed histogram in the simulation).
Idea 4

Linking the microscopic and the macroscopic

Using Newton’s laws for molecules bouncing off the walls, the kinetic model gives:

Kinetic theory equation ⭐PV = ⅓Nmc̄²
P, V: pressure and volume (macroscopic) · N: number of molecules · m: mass of one molecule · c̄² = (c₁² + c₂² + … + cN²)/N: mean square speed

Since the gas density is ρ = Nm/V, this can be written P = ⅓ρc̄². The r.m.s. speed is crms = √(c̄²).

✏️ ExampleAir at 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa has density 1.2 kg m⁻³. Find the r.m.s. speed of its molecules.
c̄² = 3P/ρ = 3(1.0 × 10⁵)/1.2 = 2.5 × 10⁵ m² s⁻²
crms = √(2.5 × 10⁵) = 500 m s⁻¹
Common mistakes ⚠️
  • m is the mass of one molecule (M/NA); Nm is the total mass.
  • c̄² is the mean of the squares, not the square of the mean speed.
Idea 5

What temperature means for an ideal gas

Compare PV = ⅓Nmc̄² with PV = nRT, and use N = nNA:

⭐⅓Nmc̄² = nRT ⇒ ½mc̄² = 3RT/(2NA)
K.E.average = 3RT/(2NA)
Total K.E. of the gas = N × K.E.avg = (3/2)nRT = (3/2)PV
r.m.s. speed: crms = √(3RT/M) (M = molar mass in kg mol⁻¹)
Interpretation ⭐
  • The absolute temperature T of an ideal gas is directly proportional to the average K.E. of its molecules.
  • Different gases at the same temperature have the same average K.E.; lighter molecules (smaller M) move faster.
  • crms depends only on T and M — not on the pressure or volume on their own.
  • At absolute zero (0 K) the average K.E. of an ideal gas would be zero.
Worked examples

Kinetic theory problems

✏️ Example 1 (helium and oxygen)1 mol of helium (M = 0.004 kg mol⁻¹) and 1 mol of oxygen (M = 0.032 kg mol⁻¹), both at 27 °C.
K.E.avg = 3(8.31)(300) ÷ [2(6.02 × 10²³)] = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J — the same for both gases.
crms(He) = √(3 × 8.31 × 300 ÷ 0.004) = 1370 m s⁻¹; crms(O₂) = √(3 × 8.31 × 300 ÷ 0.032) = 483 m s⁻¹
Ratio = √(32/4) = √8 = 2.83
✏️ Example 2 (changing temperature)The r.m.s. speed of a gas is 500 m s⁻¹ at 25 °C. Find it at 40 °C.
crms ∝ √T ⇒ c' = 500 × √(313/298) = 512 m s⁻¹

✏️ Example 3 (internal energy) 1 mol of an ideal gas is heated by 40 K at constant volume. Increase in total K.E. = (3/2)nRΔT = 1.5 × 8.31 × 40 = 499 J
Try it!

Simulation: molecules in a box

120 sample molecules move randomly in a cubic box (each stands for many real molecules). The pressure is measured by adding up the impulses of the molecules hitting the walls, and compared with the kinetic theory prediction. (Motion slowed down about 5000 times.)

Pressure measured from wall hits
⅓Nmc̄²/V = nRT/V
c_rms = √(3RT/M)
K.E. average = 3RT/(2N_A)

Things to try
  • Let it run: the measured pressure settles at nRT/V.
  • Change helium to oxygen at the same T, n and V: the molecules are slower but heavier — the pressure and the average K.E. stay the same.
  • Double the volume: the molecules hit the walls less often, so the pressure halves.

Idea 6

Real gases and ideal gases

Ideal gasReal gas
forces between moleculesnone (except in collisions)weak attractions → can condense into a liquid when cooled enough
volume of moleculesnegligiblesmall but finite
When does a real gas behave like an ideal gas? ⭐At high temperature and low pressure. Then the molecules are fast and far apart, so the attractive forces and the molecules’ own volume are negligible. At low temperature or high pressure, real gases deviate from PV = nRT (and may liquefy).
