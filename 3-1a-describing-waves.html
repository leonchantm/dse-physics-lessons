Lesson 3.1a: Describing Waves (描述波動)

Ripples on a pond, a vibrating guitar string, sound and light are all waves. In this lesson you learn what a wave is and the words physicists use to describe one.

Key wordShort meaning
wave (波)A travelling disturbance that carries energy without carrying matter.
transverse / longitudinal (橫波 / 縱波)Particles vibrate perpendicular / parallel to the direction of travel.
wavelength λ (波長)Length of one complete waveform (m).
frequency f (頻率)Number of complete waves produced per second (Hz).
wave speed v (波速)Distance travelled by the wave per second: v = fλ.

Idea 1

Waves are produced by oscillations

Flick one end of a long rope. The first piece of rope is pulled up and down; it pulls its neighbour, which pulls the next one, and so on. A pulse travels along the rope. Keep shaking and a continuous wave travels away from the source.

  • Waves are produced by oscillations (vibrations) of a source.
  • Each particle of the medium only oscillates about its equilibrium position; it does not travel with the wave.
  • The energy of the source is passed from particle to particle.
Key idea ⭐A wave transmits energy without transferring matter.
🔬 EvidenceA cork floating on a pond bobs up and down when ripples pass; it is not carried to the shore. A ribbon tied on a slinky spring moves to and fro but stays at the same place on the spring.
Common mistake ⚠️“The water moves towards the shore with the wave.” No — the waveform and the energy move; the water particles only oscillate.
Idea 2

Transverse and longitudinal waves

Transverse wave ⭐Particles vibrate perpendicular to the direction the wave travels.
Examples: waves on a string, water surface waves (treated as transverse), light and all electromagnetic waves.
Longitudinal wave ⭐Particles vibrate parallel to the direction the wave travels, forming compressions and rarefactions.
Examples: sound, a slinky spring pushed to and fro along its length.
Mechanical wavesElectromagnetic (EM) waves
Need a medium (solid, liquid or gas): sound, water waves, waves on strings.Can travel through a vacuum: light, radio waves, X-rays…

Waves that move away from the source are travelling (progressive) waves. Waves whose pattern stays in one place are stationary waves (Lesson 3.4).

Idea 3

The vocabulary of waves

TermMeaningUnit
equilibrium positionWhere a particle rests when there is no wave.—
displacementDistance (with direction) of a particle from its equilibrium position.m
crest / troughHighest / lowest points of a transverse wave.—
compression / rarefactionRegions where particles are closest together / farthest apart in a longitudinal wave.—
amplitude AMaximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium position.m
wavelength λShortest distance over which the wave repeats: crest to next crest, or compression to next compression.m
period TTime for one complete oscillation of a particle = time to produce one complete wave.s
frequency fNumber of complete waves produced (oscillations) per second.Hz = s⁻¹
wave speed vDistance the waveform travels per second.m s⁻¹
wavefrontA line joining neighbouring points that are in phase (e.g. all points on one crest).—
phaseThe stage a particle has reached in its oscillation (more in Lesson 3.1b).—
Common mistakes ⚠️
  • Amplitude is measured from the equilibrium position to a crest — not from crest to trough (that is 2A).
  • In a longitudinal wave the particles at the centre of a compression or rarefaction have zero displacement (Lesson 3.1b).

Idea 4

The wave equation

⭐ Two equations to rememberf = 1/T
In one period the wave travels one wavelength, so v = λ/T, i.e. v = fλ

Unit check (dimension analysis): Hz × m = s⁻¹ × m = m s⁻¹ ✓

What decides f, v and λ? ⭐
  • Frequency depends only on the source. It does not change when the wave enters another medium.
  • Wave speed depends only on the medium. Shaking harder (larger amplitude) or faster (higher f) does not change v.
  • So λ = v/f changes if either changes: in the same medium, doubling f halves λ.
Waves on a stretched string or spring ⭐The wave travels faster when
  • the tension is larger (stretch the spring more), or
  • the string/spring is lighter (smaller mass per unit length).
Amplitude and frequency do not affect the speed.
Common mistake ⚠️“Increasing the frequency makes the wave go faster.” In the same medium v is fixed; a higher f just gives a shorter λ.
Worked examples

Using f = 1/T and v = fλ

✏️ Example 1: ripple tankA dipper vibrating at 5.0 Hz makes ripples. The distance from the 1st crest to the 6th crest is 50 cm. Find λ, v and the time for a crest to travel 2.0 m.
1st → 6th crest = 5 wavelengths ⇒ λ = 50 ÷ 5 = 10 cm
v = fλ = 5.0 × 0.10 = 0.50 m s⁻¹
t = 2.0 ÷ 0.50 = 4.0 s
✏️ Example 2: a huge water waveWater waves of wavelength 100 m and frequency 2 Hz travel 1500 km across an ocean. Estimate the time taken.
v = fλ = 2 × 100 = 200 m s⁻¹
t = 1.5 × 10⁶ ÷ 200 = 7500 s ≈ 2 hours
Counting trap ⚠️From the 1st to the 6th crest there are 5 wavelengths, not 6.

Experiment

Measuring the speed of a wave

🔬 A pulse on a slinky spring
  1. Stretch a slinky spring to a length L along the floor; one end is held fixed.
  2. Send a single pulse from the other end and start a stop-watch.
  3. Stop the watch when the pulse returns after reflection at the fixed end; the pulse travels 2L.
  4. v = 2L/t. Repeat and take the average (or time several round trips) to reduce timing errors.
  5. Repeat with the spring stretched more (higher tension), or with a heavier spring, to see how v changes.
🔬 Water waves in a ripple tank
  1. Set a straight vibrator at a known frequency f.
  2. View the waves with a stroboscope flashing at the same frequency so the pattern looks stationary (“frozen”).
  3. Measure the distance across several wavefronts on the screen and divide to find λ (allow for the magnification of the projected image).
  4. v = fλ.
Results to expectSpeed increases with tension and decreases for a heavier spring; it does not depend on the amplitude of the pulse.
