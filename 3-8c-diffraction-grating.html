Lesson 3.8c: The Diffraction Grating (衍射光柵)

What happens with hundreds of slits instead of two? The bright fringes become sharp, bright lines at large angles. That is why gratings are used to measure wavelengths and to split light into spectra.

📌 Extension componentThe grating equation d sin θ = nλ is underlined in the C&A Guide: using it to solve problems belongs to the extension part of the Compulsory Part. Describing the grating pattern is core.
Key wordShort meaning
diffraction grating (衍射光柵)A slide with many (hundreds per mm) equally spaced, parallel slits.
grating spacing d (光柵間距)Distance between neighbouring slits: d = 1 mm ÷ (lines per mm).
order n (級數)0 for the straight-through beam, 1, 2, 3 … outwards on each side.
angle of diffraction θ (衍射角)Angle between the nth-order beam and the straight-through direction.

Idea 1

The pattern from a grating

  • Light at normal incidence passes through many narrow, equally spaced slits; each slit diffracts the light and all the waves interfere.
  • Bright lines appear only in directions where light from every slit arrives in phase, so the lines are very sharp and bright with wide dark gaps.
  • The lines are at large angles, so on a flat screen they are not evenly spaced.
  • The lines spread out more when the wavelength is longer or the grating spacing is smaller (more lines per mm).

Exam trap ⚠️For a grating the angles are large, so do not use Δy = λD/a. Use d sin θ = nλ, then y = D tan θ for a position on a screen.
Idea 2 Extension

The grating equation

Grating equation (normal incidence)d sin θ = nλ
d = grating spacing, θ = angle of the nth-order maximum from the straight-through direction, n = 0, 1, 2, …
Grating spacingd = 1 mm ÷ N (N lines per mm)
e.g. 500 lines per mm: d = 10⁻³/500 = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m
3.0 × 10⁵ lines per m: d = 1/(3.0 × 10⁵) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m
Exampleλ = 600 nm, 500 lines per mm (d = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m).
n = 1: sin θ₁ = 600 × 10⁻⁹/2.0 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.30 ⇒ θ₁ = 17.5°
n = 2: sin θ₂ = 0.60 ⇒ θ₂ = 36.9°
n = 3: sin θ₃ = 0.90 ⇒ θ₃ = 64.2°
On a screen 1.0 m away: y₁ = 1.0 tan 17.5° = 0.31 m, y₂ = 0.75 m: not evenly spaced.
Note ⭐θ is not proportional to λ (sin θ is). Doubling n does not double θ.

Idea 3 Extension

How many orders? White-light spectra

Maximum orderθ cannot exceed 90°, so sin θ ≤ 1 ⇒ nλ ≤ d ⇒ n ≤ d/λ.
nmax = the whole number part of d/λ. Total number of bright lines = 2nmax + 1 (both sides plus the centre).
Example500 lines per mm, 600 nm: d/λ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶/6.0 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.33 ⇒ nmax = 3 ⇒ 7 bright lines. (If d/λ is exactly a whole number, that order would be at 90° and cannot be seen.)
White light ⭐
  • n = 0: white (all colours go straight through).
  • Each order is a spectrum, violet inside, red outside (red has the longest λ ⇒ largest θ).
  • Higher orders are wider and dimmer.
  • The 2nd and 3rd orders always overlap: 2 × 700 nm = 1400 nm > 3 × 400 nm = 1200 nm, so red of order 2 goes beyond violet of order 3.

Worked examples Extension

Worked examples

Example 1: find λ, then predictA grating with 300 lines per mm gives a first-order maximum at 10.4°.
d = 10⁻³/300 = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m (1M)
λ = d sin θ = (3.33 × 10⁻⁶) sin 10.4° = 6.02 × 10⁻⁷ m (1A)
nmax: d/λ = 5.54 ⇒ 5 ⇒ 11 bright lines.
Example 2: width of a spectrumWhite light (400–700 nm), 400 lines per mm (d = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m), screen 1.0 m away. First-order spectrum width?
violet: sin θ = 0.16 ⇒ 9.21°; red: sin θ = 0.28 ⇒ 16.26°
width = 1.0(tan 16.26° − tan 9.21°) = 0.292 − 0.162 = 0.130 m.
Example 3: from a known orderThird-order beams appear at 45°. Highest order?
d sin 45° = 3λ ⇒ d/λ = 3/sin 45° = 4.24 ⇒ 4th order.
Example 4: coinciding linesLight of 750 nm and 500 nm passes through a grating. 2 × 750 = 3 × 500 = 1500 nm, so the 2nd order of 750 nm coincides with the 3rd order of 500 nm. With 400 lines per mm: sin θ = 1500 × 10⁻⁹/2.5 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.60 ⇒ θ = 36.9°.
Experiment

Experiment: estimating the wavelength of light with a grating

Method A: spectrometer-free (two metre rules)
  1. Place a lamp with a colour filter at the end of a metre rule; put a grating at the other end, L = 1.00 m away, facing the lamp.
  2. Fix a second metre rule perpendicular to the first, at the lamp.
  3. Looking through the grating, have a partner move a pencil along the second rule until it lines up with the first-order image; read its distance x from the lamp.
  4. Repeat on the other side and average; repeat for the second order.
  5. tan θ = x/L; λ = d sin θ / n.

e.g. x = 0.200 m, L = 1.00 m, 3.0 × 10⁵ lines per m ⇒ θ = 11.3°, λ = 6.54 × 10⁻⁷ m (red). Second order would be at θ = 23.1°, x = 0.426 m.

Method B: laser and screenShine a laser normally on the grating; measure D and the distance between the two first-order spots (2y); θ = tan⁻¹(y/D); λ = d sin θ.
Accuracy tips ⭐
  • Measure to orders on both sides and halve: removes error from the grating not being exactly normal.
  • Higher orders give larger angles ⇒ smaller percentage error, but they are dimmer.
  • Never look directly into a laser.
