Lesson 3.9a: Sound Waves (聲波)

Sound is a wave you can’t see. You will find out what kind of wave it is, why it needs a medium, which frequencies we can hear, and how sound reflects, refracts, diffracts and interferes like other waves.

Key wordShort meaning
longitudinal wave (縱波)Particles vibrate parallel to the direction of travel.
compression / rarefaction (密部 / 疏部)Regions where the particles are close together / far apart.
audible frequency range (可聽頻率範圍)About 20 Hz to 20 kHz for humans.
ultrasound (超聲波)Sound above 20 kHz.
echo (回聲)Reflected sound.

Idea 1

What is sound?

  • A vibrating source (loudspeaker cone, tuning fork, vocal cords) pushes and pulls the air.
  • Cone moves out → particles pushed together → compression; cone moves in → particles spread → rarefaction.
  • The air particles vibrate back and forth along the direction of travel: sound is a longitudinal wave. A candle flame in front of a loudspeaker flickers to and fro, not up and down.
Sound needs a medium ⭐Sound is a mechanical wave: it is passed on by particles. It cannot travel through a vacuum. Bell-jar experiment: as the air is pumped out, the ringing bell becomes fainter, though you can still see the hammer striking.
MediumSpeed of sound / m s⁻¹
air (20 °C)≈ 340
water≈ 1500
aluminium≈ 6400

In general sound is fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases; in air it is faster when warmer.

Idea 2

Audible sound and ultrasound

Audible frequency rangeHumans hear about 20 Hz to 20 kHz. The range differs between people; the upper limit falls with age. You can find your own range with a signal generator and a loudspeaker, slowly raising the frequency until you can no longer hear it.
Wavelengths in air (v = 340 m s⁻¹)20 Hz: λ = 340/20 = 17 m
20 kHz: λ = 340/20 000 = 0.017 m = 1.7 cm
So sound wavelengths are similar in size to everyday objects.
Ultrasound (> 20 kHz) ⭐Too high for humans to hear, but many animals (dogs, bats, dolphins) can. Uses:
  • Sonar: measure sea depth or find fish by timing echoes.
  • Medical scanning: imaging a foetus or organs (safer than X-rays).
  • Industry: detecting cracks/flaws in metal; ultrasonic cleaning.
Don’t confuse ⚠️Ultrasound is a sound wave (longitudinal, needs a medium). Ultraviolet is an EM wave.

Idea 3

Sound reflects, refracts, diffracts and interferes

Reflection: echoesEcho distance: the sound goes there and back, so 2d = vt. Used in sonar and in measuring the speed of sound. Concert halls use soft materials to reduce unwanted echoes.
RefractionSound changes speed (and direction) in air layers at different temperatures. At night the air near the ground is cooler, so sound bends back towards the ground and distant sounds are heard more clearly. Frequency stays the same; λ changes with speed.
DiffractionSound wavelengths (cm to m) are similar to doorways and gaps between buildings, so sound bends round corners. You can hear people in a corridor before you see them: light (λ ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁷ m) hardly diffracts there.
InterferenceTwo loudspeakers driven by the same signal generator are coherent. Walking across in front of them you hear loud and soft positions: path difference nλ → loud; (n + ½)λ → soft. Noise-cancelling headphones add an antiphase sound to cancel noise.

Simulation: sound in a medium, and echoes

Particles mode: each line is a layer of particles in front of a loudspeaker (slow motion). Echo mode: a clap travels to a wall and back; record the echo times for different distances.

Speed v

Things to try
  • Particles mode: double the frequency. What happens to the distance between compressions?
  • Change the medium from air to water at the same frequency. What changes, what stays the same?
  • Choose vacuum. What happens?
  • Echo mode: record echo times for 4–5 distances. What does the slope of the 2d–t graph give?

Idea 4

Comparing sound waves and light waves

LightSound
type of waveelectromagnetic, transversemechanical, longitudinal
mediumnot needed (travels through a vacuum)needed (cannot travel through a vacuum)
speed3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in a vacuum; slower in water and glass≈ 340 m s⁻¹ in air; faster in liquids and solids
gas → liquidslows down, bends towards the normalspeeds up, bends away from the normal
wavelength400–700 nm: much smaller than everyday objects≈ 2 cm to 17 m: similar to everyday objects
diffraction in daily lifehardly noticedeasily noticed (round corners)
common propertiesboth show reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference; both obey v = fλ
Thunder and lightning ⭐Light arrives almost instantly; sound takes about 3 s per km. A 6 s gap means the storm is about 340 × 6 ≈ 2 km away.
Worked examples

Worked examples

Example 1: echoA girl shouts towards a cliff and hears the echo 0.80 s later (v = 340 m s⁻¹). Distance to the cliff?
2d = vt (1M) ⇒ d = (340)(0.80)/2 = 136 m (1A)
Example 2: sonarA ship sends 40 kHz ultrasound down; the echo returns after 2.0 s (v = 1500 m s⁻¹ in sea water).
depth = (1500)(2.0)/2 = 1500 m; λ = v/f = 1500/40 000 = 0.0375 m.
Example 3: change of mediumSound of wavelength 25 cm in air (340 m s⁻¹) enters a liquid where its wavelength is 1.0 m.
f = 340/0.25 = 1360 Hz (unchanged) ⇒ v = fλ = 1360 × 1.0 = 1360 m s⁻¹.
Example 4: speed of sound by clapping (experiment)Stand 50 m from a wall and clap so that each clap coincides with the echo of the previous one. Time 21 claps (20 intervals) = 6.0 s.
t = 0.30 s for 2 × 50 m ⇒ v = 100/0.30 = 333 m s⁻¹. Timing many intervals reduces the reaction-time error.
Graphs in speed-of-sound experiments ⚠️Plot 2d (y) against t (x): a straight line through the origin shows 2d ∝ t, and slope = v. A straight line that does not pass through the origin is only “linearly increasing”, not “directly proportional”: say which one the data show.
